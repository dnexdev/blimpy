1. What you are building

A 1.1 m latex balloon carries a small gondola directly under it. The gondola has four little propellers and an ESP32 that only does reflexes: mix the four motors, hold the yaw rate, and cut power if the laptop goes quiet. Two cameras in the room see the balloon and the person; the laptop does everything else and sends four numbers to the gondola twenty times a second.

Why four motors, all reversible

A sphere has no keel. After a turn it keeps sliding the old way, and at these speeds drag will not stop it in time; only thrust does. So the two rear motors drive, brake, and steer; a sideways motor through the centre kills sideways drift; a vertical motor holds height, because lift changes by grams as the hall warms and helium leaks. Ballast alone puts it in the ceiling within a minute.

Trim the balloon about one gram heavy. On power loss it settles to the floor instead of the ceiling, and the vertical motor mostly pushes up, which is its efficient direction.

2. Motor layout, plan view

Prop direction matters because the props are fixed. A propeller gives about 60% thrust in reverse. Choose each motor's spin direction so the efficient direction is the one used most: forward for L and R, up for V. S is used both ways; either is fine. The 8520 set comes with clockwise and counter-clockwise props; put a CW and a CCW on L and R so their torques cancel.

3. Wiring

Link changed. The gondola runs the hardware team's Bluetooth firmware: it advertises as BalloonRobot, takes per-motor percentages (MOTORS c d e f, STOP) and streams IMU lines. The laptop bridge laptop/control/ble_gondola.py runs the mixer and the failsafe. The pin table below is the legacy WiFi build; the motor roles (L and R rear, S sideways, V vertical) and the plan view still apply. Map C/D/E/F to L/R/S/V in config.BLE on the bench (README section 3).

One ESP32-C3 SuperMini drives two DRV8833 dual H-bridge boards. Each motor uses one bridge channel as PWM plus direction, because the C3 has only six PWM outputs. Both driver boards share one enable line, which is also the onboard LED, so LED lit means motors disabled.

The single most common "nothing spins". The DRV8833's STBY pin must be pulled high or the chip sleeps. Some boards label it EEP or nSLEEP. It is tied to GPIO 8 here so the firmware can cut power hard; if a motor test fails, check that pin first, then the motor supply, then the shared ground.

PWM details, for whoever wires the drivers

  • PWM at 25 kHz, 10 bit, above hearing. Duty is clamped to 50% in the mixer; the laptop caps it further.
  • Positive value: DIR low, duty = value. Negative value: DIR high, duty = 1 − |value|. That is the DRV8833 fast-decay and slow-decay convention; it is handled in firmware.
  • Every motor ramps by at most 5% per 20 ms tick, so a full stop to 50% takes 0.2 s. This keeps current spikes under the protected cell's cut-off.
  • Add 0.1 µF ceramic capacitors across each motor's terminals to tame brush noise on the I2C bus.

The lift margin is large. Do not chase grams; chase balance and rigidity. A floppy frame turns motor thrust into wobble, and the heading estimator reads wobble as noise.

5. Build sequence

Bench first, balloon last. Everything up to step 8 happens on a table with the gondola tied down. The one rule: never plug USB into the ESP32 while the battery switch is on.

  1. Solder the driver headers and the power tree

    Solder the header pins on both DRV8833 boards; pushed-in pins are the reason motor tests fail intermittently. Wire battery + through the switch to a small bus: from there to VM on both driver boards and to the regulator input. The 1000 µF goes across the battery after the switch, a 100 µF across each board's VM and GND. Regulator output to the ESP32's 3V3 pin, not the 5V pin. One ground bus shared by everything.

  2. Charge the cell and check the rail

    Charge on the TP4056 until its light changes. Switch on with nothing else connected and measure: battery 3.9 to 4.2 V at VM, 3.3 V at the ESP32 pin. If the regulator sags below 3.2 V when the ESP32 boots, it is too small.

  3. Confirm the board is alive on the network

    Battery off, USB in. Run the serial monitor and watch it join the hotspot and print its IP and name. Then teleop from the laptop:

    python -m laptop.control.ble_gondola   # terminal 1: connects to BalloonRobot
python -m laptop.control.teleop        # terminal 2

    LED fast blink turns to slow blink when commands arrive. SPACE arms: LED goes off. Ctrl+C: LED back on within half a second. That is the failsafe, and it already works on this board.

  4. One motor, then all four

    Wire motor L only on board A, STBY to GPIO 8. Battery on, USB out. Arm, press w: it spins. Press s: it reverses. No spin means STBY, then VM, then ground, in that order. Then add the rest. Teleop prints the four duties on screen; each key should move exactly the motor the layout drawing says. If a motor runs backwards, swap its two output wires at the driver.

  5. Mount the IMU and check the sign

    Glue the MPU6050 flat to the gondola with its chip facing up and its printed X arrow pointing forward. Set HAS_IMU 1 in the firmware config, reflash, keep the gondola still for two seconds after boot. Rotate it counter-clockwise by hand seen from above: the telemetry yaw must increase. If it decreases, the chip is upside down.

  6. Build the frame

    A light cross: a 25 cm rear bar carrying L and R at its ends with thrust lines forward and parallel, a central body for the electronics, the S motor bolted transverse at the body's centre, and V under the middle with its prop clear by at least one prop radius. Put the battery, the heaviest part, at the centre so the centre of mass sits under the harness point. Every motor sits on soft foam tape; it is the difference between silent and buzzing.

  7. Balance the props and weigh the gondola

    Spin each prop on a pin; sand the heavy blade's tip until it stops settling to one side. Weigh the finished gondola with battery; write the number on it. It goes into the simulator constants later.

  8. Full bench run, tied down

    Tether the gondola to something heavy. Arm, run each axis to the 50% cap, then Ctrl+C the teleop and watch everything stop within half a second. Check the battery voltage at VM during the run: below 3.5 V under load means the protection board is clamping, and the throttle cap must come down.

  9. Inflate the balloon

    Inflate slowly to a circumference of 3.46 m, which is 1.10 m across. Tie off, then tie a light harness of two or three strings around the neck. The envelope is thin: no rings, no watches, no rough tables. Optional Hi-Float gel slows the leak for a long demo day.

  10. Hang the gondola and trim

    Hang the gondola from the neck harness as close under the balloon as the strings allow, level. Add ballast, washers or putty on the gondola centre, until the whole thing sinks very slowly with the motors off: about one gram heavy. It will feel too heavy; it is not. The vertical motor holds it up with a few percent duty and the trim is relearned automatically.

  11. First flight, hover only

    Cameras calibrated (section 6), balloon in view. Start the pilot, arm. The balloon does a few short pushes to learn which way it faces, then holds position. Let it hover for a minute before anyone says "follow me".

6. The camera rig in the room

The balloon carries no camera. Two identical cameras stand in the room, look down at the demo area, and both see one printed tag on the floor that defines where "zero" is. Phones with DroidCam work now; Raspberry Pi Camera Module 3 pairs later. The scripts do not care which.

  • Lock the lens. Camera Module 3 autofocuses; both cameras must run manual focus, fixed exposure, gain and white balance, with the same settings. The streaming command in the README already sets them. Phones: lock focus and exposure in the camera app.
  • Network. Everything joins the laptop's hotspot, never the hackathon WiFi. Pi cameras prefer an Ethernet cable to the laptop.
  • Calibration ritual, per camera. Once ever: checkerboard intrinsics, one minute of waving the printed board. Every time a camera is placed: point it at the floor tag, run extrinsics, sixty seconds, keep the camera still. Then the tape-measure test: stand on a known spot, check the printed position.
  • Balloon fine-tune, once the real balloon exists. Fifty frames of it in the demo light, labelled by accepting the boxes the model already draws, then a three-minute training run. This is what makes the detector confident on a translucent white balloon with fins and a gondola.

7. Before the first flight, in one place

Go / no-go.
  • Battery switch off whenever USB is plugged in.
  • STBY on both driver boards wired to GPIO 8; LED lit means motors are disabled.
  • Each teleop key moves exactly the motor the plan view says, in the direction it says.
  • Ctrl+C on the laptop stops all motors within half a second.
  • Telemetry yaw increases when the gondola is turned counter-clockwise by hand.
  • Every motor on foam tape, props balanced, nothing touches a prop.
  • Balloon at 1.10 m, gondola level and tight under it, trimmed about one gram heavy.
  • Both cameras calibrated today, tag flat on the floor, tape-measure test within a hand's width.
  • Demo area away from vents and doors.

When the hardware is done, three numbers go back into the software: the measured gondola weight, the measured motor spacing, and the measured inflated diameter. They live at the top of the simulator's balloon class and in the vision config, and the test suite is re-run after changing them.
